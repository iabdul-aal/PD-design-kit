\chapter{Germanium-on-Silicon Photodetector Design and Simulation}
\label{ch:photodetector}

\noindent\textit{Companion presentation: a Marp slide deck summarizing this chapter is included in the repository as \texttt{thesis/chapter\_photodetector\_slides.md}, with a rendered HTML version saved alongside it.}

Waveguide-integrated germanium-on-silicon photodetectors are central to modern
silicon photonics receivers because they combine strong absorption in the telecom
bands with monolithic integration on a silicon platform~\cite{SalehTeich1991,Hecht2021,Yariv1997,Chrostowski2015,Kaminow2013VIA}. This chapter follows a
standard research-paper structure organized into three main sections: a concept
review, a literature review, and a design-and-evaluation section covering the
device architecture, trade-offs, methodology, validation, results, and outlook.
The design target is an O-band vertical n-i-p Ge-on-Si photodiode centered at
\SI{1310}{\nano\metre}, with benchmarks taken from the \SI{103}{\giga\hertz}
U-shaped-electrode photodiode of Shi \textit{et al.}~\cite{Shi2024}.

\section{Concept Review}
\label{sec:pd_concept}

\subsection{Performance Targets for O-Band Receivers}
Photodetectors used in short-reach optical interconnects must satisfy several
constraints at the same time: high responsivity for sensitivity, low dark current
for noise control, sufficient saturation power for linearity, and bandwidth well
above 50~GBd signaling rates~\cite{Agrawal1997FOCS,Zhong2018,ProakisSalehi2007,Pilori2019}. For the photodiode studied here, the
main figures of merit are the external responsivity,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R} = \frac{I_{\mathrm{ph}}}{P_{\mathrm{opt}}},
    \label{eq:pd_responsivity}
\end{equation}
the optical absorption,
\begin{equation}
    A = 1 - |T| - |R|,
    \label{eq:pd_absorption}
\end{equation}
and the combined transit-time and RC-limited bandwidth,
\begin{equation}
    f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1/f_{\mathrm{tt}}^2 + 1/f_{\mathrm{RC}}^2}}.
    \label{eq:pd_bandwidth}
\end{equation}

Dark current matters because it enters directly into the shot-noise floor,
\begin{equation}
    i_{n,\mathrm{shot}} = \sqrt{2 q (I_d + I_{\mathrm{ph}}) B},
    \label{eq:pd_shot_noise}
\end{equation}
so an optical improvement that significantly increases leakage may degrade the
system-level link margin. This makes Ge-on-Si photodiode design a multiphysics
problem in which optical confinement, electrostatics, carrier transport, and
parasitic circuit elements must be optimized together rather than separately~\cite{Yan2024,Alasio2024}.

\subsection{Operating Principle of the Vertical n-i-p Ge-on-Si Photodiode}
The device considered in this chapter is a waveguide-coupled vertical n-i-p
photodiode, combining integrated-waveguide optics with semiconductor carrier
transport~\cite{Iizuka2002Vol2,Basu2003,Rossler2009,AshcroftMermin2021,Kantorovich2004,Ziman1972}. Light first propagates in a silicon input waveguide, then expands
through a taper and couples into a germanium mesa where absorption generates
electron-hole pairs. The intrinsic Ge region forms the main depletion and
collection volume, the $n^{++}$-Ge top layer provides the cathode contact, and
the $p^{++}$-Si underneath provides the anode path.

In the optical domain, absorbed power density is converted into a volumetric
generation rate,
\begin{equation}
    G(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{P_{\mathrm{abs}}(\mathbf{r})}{h f},
    \label{eq:pd_generation_rate}
\end{equation}
which is then imported into the drift-diffusion simulation. This coupling is
important because the electrical response depends not only on the total absorbed
power but also on where carriers are generated inside the stack and how quickly
they can be swept out by the internal field.

\subsection{Core Design Trade-Offs}
Four trade-offs dominate the design of high-speed Ge-on-Si photodetectors.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Absorber length versus capacitance:} longer Ge mesas improve
          absorption and responsivity, but also increase junction area and RC
          loading.
    \item \textbf{Intrinsic thickness versus transit time:} thicker intrinsic Ge
          can support stronger absorption and lower capacitance density, but the
          longer carrier travel distance can reduce speed.
    \item \textbf{Contact placement versus optical perturbation:} bringing metal
          contacts closer reduces series resistance but may increase optical loss
          and field non-uniformity.
    \item \textbf{Field strength versus dark current:} stronger electric fields
          improve carrier sweep-out and bandwidth, but poorly managed junction
          fields can elevate dark current and reliability risk.
\end{itemize}

The U-shaped electrode geometry is attractive because it balances these competing
needs. It preserves a relatively large optical interaction region while reducing
series resistance and keeping capacitance low enough for \SI{100}{\giga\hertz}-class
operation.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:pd_literature}

\subsection{Evolution of Waveguide-Integrated Ge-on-Si Photodetectors}
The field of Ge-on-Si photodetection matured through a sequence of advances in
integration, speed, responsivity, and circuit compatibility. Early work by Vivien
\textit{et al.} demonstrated a \SI{42}{\giga\hertz} waveguide-integrated germanium
photodetector on SOI~\cite{Vivien2009}, while Ang \textit{et al.} showed low-thermal-budget
evanescent coupling on a Si CMOS-compatible platform~\cite{Ang2010}. These studies
established the viability of germanium as the absorber of choice for monolithic
silicon photonics photodiodes. Recent overview articles and a closely related
doctoral dissertation provide broader context for this design space~\cite{Wang2024,Yan2024,Alasio2024}.

Subsequent work expanded the design space. Ramaswamy \textit{et al.} targeted
high-power microwave-photonic applications~\cite{Ramaswamy2010}, Fang \textit{et al.}
demonstrated a vertical pin Ge-on-Si structure on a wet-etched silicon recess~\cite{Fang2013},
and Novack \textit{et al.} used inductive gain peaking to push bandwidth to
\SI{60}{\giga\hertz}~\cite{Novack2013}. Other groups emphasized low-voltage operation,
high responsivity, or polarization robustness, including the silicon-contacted
devices of Chen \textit{et al.}~\cite{Chen2015,Chen2016Si}, the high-responsivity
waveguide-coupled photodiode of Lischke \textit{et al.}~\cite{Lischke2015}, and the
polarization-insensitive lumped detector of Chen \textit{et al.}~\cite{Chen2016Pol}.

The more recent literature focuses on narrowing the remaining trade-offs between
responsivity, dark current, bandwidth, and saturation power. This includes
mode-evolution coupling for higher saturation power~\cite{Byrd2017}, lateral
Si/Ge/Si heterojunctions~\cite{Virot2017}, footprint reduction by photonic bandgap
engineering~\cite{Zhou2018}, low-voltage heterostructured waveguide detectors~\cite{Benedikovic2019},
zero-bias operation~\cite{deCea2021}, ultra-fast germanium photodiodes with bandwidths
up to \SI{265}{\giga\hertz}~\cite{Lischke2021}, parasitic-parameter engineering~\cite{Shi2021},
and receiver-level demonstrations for high-speed data links~\cite{Su2022}. The most
relevant recent reference for this chapter is the \SI{103}{\giga\hertz}
germanium-on-silicon photodiode of Shi \textit{et al.} using an optimized U-shaped
electrode~\cite{Shi2024}, while Cao \textit{et al.} illustrate the ongoing interest
in high-power Ge/Si waveguide photodetectors on the same platform~\cite{Cao2024}.

\subsection{Positioning of the Present Chapter}
Taken together, the literature shows that high-speed Ge-on-Si photodiodes are not
limited by absorption alone. The dominant challenge is achieving strong absorption,
low dark current, and large electrical bandwidth in the same layout. This chapter
therefore focuses on a vertical n-i-p architecture with a U-shaped anode path,
because that architecture directly addresses the resistance-capacitance bottleneck
identified in the recent high-speed literature while remaining compatible with the
waveguide-coupled Ge-on-Si design lineage established by earlier work.

\section{Design, Methodology, Validation, and Outlook}
\label{sec:pd_design}

\subsection{Design Objectives and Device Architecture}
The design objective is a compact O-band photodiode suitable for
53.125~GBd PAM-4 links, with target metrics aligned to the benchmark
reported in~\cite{Shi2024}: responsivity near \SI{0.95}{\ampere\per\watt}, dark
current near \SI{1.3}{\nano\ampere} at \SI{-1}{\volt}, and electrical bandwidth
above \SI{100}{\giga\hertz}. The structure is implemented on a
\SI{220}{\nano\metre} SOI platform with a germanium mesa integrated above the
silicon rib region.

The anode contact is implemented through a U-shaped metal geometry surrounding the
Ge mesa. This geometry reduces the effective current path in silicon and improves
the resistance-capacitance trade-off without placing excessive metal directly in
the optical interaction region. The optical coupling path includes a
\SI{40}{\micro\metre} adiabatic taper that expands the mode from the input silicon
waveguide into the wider Ge absorption region.

\subsection{Design Trade-Offs}
The adopted geometry follows the trade-offs discussed in \cref{sec:pd_concept}. In
particular, the Ge length is chosen to achieve high O-band absorption without
excessive junction capacitance, the intrinsic Ge thickness is selected to balance
carrier collection and transit time, and the U-shaped electrode reduces the series
resistance associated with the anode path. This means the design is not optimized
for a single metric in isolation; instead, it is tuned to reach a balanced receiver
operating point where responsivity, dark current, and bandwidth all remain within
target.

\subsection{Methodology}
\subsubsection{Optical FDTD Methodology}
Three-dimensional finite-difference time-domain simulations were performed in
Lumerical FDTD Solutions 2020 R2. The model includes the input silicon waveguide,
the \SI{40}{\micro\metre} taper, the full Ge absorber length, and the surrounding
cladding regions. A fundamental TE mode is launched at the input, with power
monitors used to record transmission and reflection so that absorption can be
computed through \cref{eq:pd_absorption}. The Ge region uses a
\SI{20}{\nano\metre} isotropic mesh override. The optical generation profile
derived from \cref{eq:pd_generation_rate} is exported to
\texttt{ge\_gen\_oband.mat} for electrical simulation.

\subsubsection{Electrical CHARGE Methodology}
Carrier transport is simulated in Lumerical DEVICE CHARGE under 3-D steady-state
operation. Silicon and germanium material models are configured with appropriate
mobility and recombination settings, and Shockley-Read-Hall recombination is
enabled in germanium with calibrated carrier lifetimes
$\tau_n = \tau_p = \SI{5}{\nano\second}$ so that the dark current matches the
published benchmark near \SI{-1}{\volt}. The cathode is reverse biased while the
anode is held at ground, and the imported optical generation map is used to
compute photocurrent under illumination.

\subsubsection{System-Level Validation Methodology}
To connect device-level behavior to link-level implications, the photodiode
response is inserted into a MATLAB-based PAM-4 receiver flow operating at
53.125~GBd. The model includes pulse shaping, bandwidth limitation,
shot noise, thermal noise, matched filtering, and decision-threshold extraction.
This system-level step does not replace device validation, but it confirms that the
simulated photodiode can act as a realistic receiver front end rather than only as
an isolated device~\cite{Agrawal1997FOCS,Zhong2018,ProakisSalehi2007,Pilori2019}.

\subsection{Validation Strategy}
Validation is performed at three levels. First, the optical solver is checked by
verifying that the chosen Ge length delivers the expected O-band absorption. Second,
the drift-diffusion solver is benchmarked against published dark current,
responsivity, and bandwidth targets from~\cite{Shi2024}. Third, the exported compact
response is exercised in a PAM-4 receiver chain to verify that the device-level
metrics remain meaningful under a communication-system workload. In other words,
the validation loop progresses from optical absorption, to carrier transport, to
system-level eye opening and level separation.

\subsection{Results and Discussion}
\subsubsection{Optical Results}
The Ge mesa length was swept from \SIrange{2}{8}{\micro\metre} to identify the
shortest absorber that still provides near-complete absorption at
\SI{1310}{\nano\metre}. This sweep captures the fundamental optical versus
capacitance trade-off: beyond a certain length, additional absorption becomes
incremental while the electrical penalty continues to grow.

The optical generation-rate map in \cref{fig:gen_rate_map} shows how absorbed power
is distributed across the active region. This distribution matters because it sets
the initial condition for carrier generation in the electrical solver.

Overall, the results show that the chosen architecture is not only optically
absorptive but also electrically credible. The main value of the simulation flow is
that it links optical design decisions directly to internal electrostatics and then
to communication-level behavior.

\subsection{Outlook}
Several extensions would strengthen this work further. First, the compact model can
be expanded to include temperature dependence, process corners, and packaging
parasitics so that link-level simulations capture more of the real implementation
environment. Second, experimental validation should be added to confirm the
calibrated Shockley-Read-Hall lifetime assumptions and the extracted parasitic
parameters. Third, the same workflow can be extended toward high-power operation,
alternative junction geometries, and more advanced receiver co-design. The present
chapter therefore serves both as a device study and as a reusable methodology for
future Ge-on-Si photodetector development.

The scripts, generated data products, and thesis-ready figures for this chapter are
maintained inside the same repository so that the workflow remains reproducible from
FDTD, through CHARGE, to the system-level MATLAB validation stage.
