\section[Photodetector Architectures]{Photodetector Architectures}
\label{sec:pd_architectures}

With the metrics of \cref{sec:pd_metrics} as the evaluation framework, the
landscape of photodetector device classes can be surveyed systematically. Each
architecture is assessed against responsivity, bandwidth, and noise, and each
is found to resolve one specific limitation of its predecessor, until the
waveguide-integrated PIN junction emerges not as a historical preference but as
the consequence of removing every identified obstacle in sequence.

\subsection[PN and PIN Junctions]{PN and PIN Junctions: Depletion Engineering}
\label{subsec:pd_pn_pin}

\paragraph{Photoconductive Detection.}
The photoconductor is the simplest optical transducer because it requires no
junction and no depletion engineering. Its apparent advantage is internal gain:
\begin{equation}
    G_{\mathrm{PC}} = \frac{\tau_r}{\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}},
    \qquad
    \tau_{\mathrm{tr}} = \frac{L^2}{\mu_n V_b},
    \label{eq:pc_gain}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_r$ is the recombination lifetime and $\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}$ is the
carrier transit time between the ohmic contacts. The problem is that the same
lifetime that enables gain also fixes the bandwidth:
\begin{equation}
    f_{3\,\mathrm{dB,PC}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\tau_r}.
    \label{eq:pc_bw}
\end{equation}
For germanium, a lifetime in the microsecond class implies a bandwidth in the
kilohertz-to-megahertz regime, which is many orders of magnitude below the
\SI{100}{\giga\baud}-class target of the present work. The photoconductor is
therefore rejected by the gain-bandwidth product before any detailed geometry
optimisation is attempted.

\paragraph{PN Junction Photodiodes.}
The PN junction fixes the most serious weakness of the photoconductor by creating
an internal electric field that sweeps carriers rather than waiting for them to
diffuse across a biased resistor. Yet the PN device still retains a slow
component because photocarriers generated in the quasi-neutral regions must
diffuse back to the depletion edge. The resulting response can be written as:
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{PN}}(f) =
    H_{\mathrm{drift}}(f)\,
    \frac{1}{1+j2\pi f\tau_D},
    \label{eq:hpn}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_D$ is the diffusion-tail time constant. At moderate symbol rates this
penalty is manageable; at the \SIrange{50}{100}{\giga\hertz} electrical bandwidth
class it is not. The PN device therefore solves the photoconductor's speed
mechanism only partially.

\paragraph{PIN Photodiodes.}
The PIN structure removes that residual limitation by inserting an intrinsic layer
and forcing the useful optical generation to occur inside a fully depleted region.
Once the full intrinsic thickness is depleted, the electric field is approximately
uniform:
\begin{equation}
    E(x) \approx \frac{|V_{\mathrm{bias}}|+V_{\mathrm{bi}}}{W_i},
    \qquad x\in[0,W_i].
    \label{eq:pin_efield_uniform}
\end{equation}
The diffusion tail of \cref{eq:hpn} is then suppressed and the bandwidth is
reduced to the competition already formalised through
\cref{eq:bw_tt,eq:bw_rc}: transit time improves when $W_i$ shrinks, whereas the
RC penalty worsens when the junction footprint grows. The PIN structure also
provides independent control of the depletion width via the i-layer thickness,
decoupling it from the doping levels used to define the ohmic contacts.
That is the key structural reason the PIN, rather than the photoconductor or
the simple PN diode, remains the default high-speed receiver device.

\subsection[Avalanche Photodiodes]{Avalanche Photodiodes: Gain versus Noise}
\label{subsec:pd_apd}

An avalanche photodiode appears attractive because it multiplies the responsivity:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{APD}} = M\,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{PIN}},
    \label{eq:r_apd}
\end{equation}
but that gain is not free. The avalanche build-up time lowers the usable
bandwidth as $M$ increases, introducing the gain-bandwidth product (GBP)
$M \times f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}} = \mathrm{const}$ as a fundamental constraint.
The excess noise factor $F(M)$ additionally penalises the SNR: while ideal
multiplication with $F=1$ adds no extra noise, realistic semiconductor avalanche
with near-equal ionisation coefficients for electrons and holes (as in Ge) drives
$F\rightarrow 2M$, rapidly erasing the gain advantage in the shot-noise-limited
sensitivity formula of \cref{eq:sens_full}. At the present operating point, the
receiver needs the bandwidth headroom more urgently than it needs internal gain,
which is why the low-bias PIN remains preferable to the higher-voltage APD.

\subsection[Waveguide-Integrated Photodetectors]{Waveguide-Integrated Photodetectors: Interaction Length Advantage}
\label{subsec:pd_waveguide}

The PIN junction resolves the device-level bandwidth problem but does not, by
itself, resolve the geometric conflict between absorption and transit time.
In a surface-illuminated PIN, the same dimension, namely the absorber thickness
$W_i$, must satisfy both the Beer-Lambert absorption requirement
($W_i\gtrsim\alpha^{-1}$ for high $\mathcal{A}$) and the transit-time
requirement ($W_i$ small for high $f_{\mathrm{tt}}$ via \cref{eq:bw_tt}). At
\SI{1310}{\nano\metre}, where $\alpha^{-1}\approx\SI{4.8}{\micro\metre}$ in Ge,
these two requirements are directly contradictory. Waveguide integration resolves
this conflict by changing the geometry.

In a waveguide-integrated photodetector, the optical mode is confined laterally
by the waveguide structure and propagates longitudinally along the device.
The relevant dimension for absorption is the longitudinal length $L$ rather than
the vertical thickness $W_i$. The Beer-Lambert law along the propagation direction
gives:
\begin{equation}
    P(z) = P_0\,e^{-\Gamma\alpha z},
    \label{eq:wg_beer_lambert}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma$ is the mode confinement factor, defined as the fraction of
optical power confined within the absorbing Ge region. The total absorption efficiency
approaches:
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{\mathrm{abs}} = 1 - e^{-\Gamma\alpha L},
    \label{eq:wg_abs_efficiency}
\end{equation}
which can approach unity by increasing $L$, entirely independently of the
vertical thickness $W_i$. This geometric orthogonality is the decisive
architectural advantage: $L$ controls $\mathcal{R}$ through
\cref{eq:wg_abs_efficiency}, while $W_i$ controls $f_{\mathrm{tt}}$ through
\cref{eq:bw_tt}, and the two parameters can be optimised separately.

The PIN is chosen because it eliminates the gain-bandwidth constraint of the APD,
removes the diffusion tail of the PN junction, imposes no inherent bandwidth
ceiling from gain mechanisms, and pairs naturally with an external transimpedance
amplifier (TIA) whose thermal noise is the dominant system noise source in
high-speed receivers. Waveguide integration restores geometric orthogonality
between the absorption dimension and the carrier collection dimension. The
remaining design problem, namely achieving both high responsivity and high
bandwidth simultaneously within the waveguide-integrated PIN framework, is the
tension addressed by the material choice of \cref{sec:pd_material}, the electrode
engineering of \cref{sec:pd_design}, and the literature context of
\cref{sec:pd_literature}.
