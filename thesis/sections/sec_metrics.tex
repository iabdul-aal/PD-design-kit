\section[Performance Metrics]{Performance Metrics and Design Trade-offs}
\label{sec:pd_metrics}

The transport and absorption physics of \cref{sec:pd_fundamentals} support a set
of engineering metrics that define what a useful photodetector must accomplish and
at what cost. These metrics are not independent: they form a multi-dimensional
trade-off space in which improving one quantity typically degrades another. Every
architectural and material choice in the sections that follow is justifiable
using the definitions established here.

\subsection[Responsivity and Quantum Efficiency]{Responsivity and Quantum Efficiency}
\label{subsec:pd_responsivity}

\subsubsection{Physical Basis and Definition}

The fundamental function of a photodetector is to transduce an optical power flux
into a measurable electrical current. This transduction is characterised by the
\emph{external responsivity} $\mathcal{R}$, defined as the ratio of the
generated photocurrent $I_{\mathrm{ph}}$ to the incident optical power
$P_{\mathrm{opt}}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R} \equiv \frac{I_{\mathrm{ph}}}{P_{\mathrm{opt}}}
    \quad [\si{\ampere\per\watt}].
    \label{eq:responsivity}
\end{equation}
The qualifier \emph{external} is important: $\mathcal{R}$ accounts for all
loss processes that occur between the guided optical mode incident upon the
device and the photocurrent delivered to the readout circuit. It therefore
includes coupling loss from the silicon waveguide into the germanium absorber,
reflection at the Si/Ge interface, incomplete absorption along the device
length, and any carrier recombination prior to collection at the contacts.

The upper bound on responsivity is derived from energy conservation. If every
incident photon of energy $h\nu = hc/\lambda$ were to generate exactly one
electron–hole pair and that pair were collected without loss, then the
photocurrent per unit optical power would be:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}_{\max} = \frac{q\lambda}{hc}
    \approx \SI{1.057}{\ampere\per\watt}
    \quad\text{at \SI{1310}{\nano\metre}},
    \label{eq:rmax}
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the elementary charge, $h$ is Planck's constant, and $c$ is the
speed of light in vacuum. The linear dependence of $\mathcal{R}_{\max}$ on
wavelength $\lambda$ is physically transparent: longer-wavelength photons carry
less energy per quantum, so generating one electron of charge $q$ requires less
power, yielding a larger current per watt. At the O-band centre wavelength of
\SI{1310}{\nano\metre}, this ceiling is approximately \SI{1.06}{\ampere\per\watt}.
Real devices fall meaningfully below this value, and the gap between $\mathcal{R}$
and $\mathcal{R}_{\max}$ is the principal object of optimisation in the optical
coupling and absorber geometry.

\subsubsection{Quantum Efficiency: External and Internal}

The responsivity can be normalised to the single-photon limit through the concept
of quantum efficiency. The \emph{external quantum efficiency} (EQE) $\eta_{\mathrm{ext}}$
is the ratio of the rate of collected electrons to the rate of photons incident
on the device, and is directly related to responsivity by:
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{\mathrm{ext}}
    = \mathcal{R}\cdot\frac{hc}{q\lambda}.
    \label{eq:eqe}
\end{equation}
This quantity captures all loss mechanisms between the input waveguide facet and
the electrical output terminal. It is the primary figure of merit at the system
level because it directly sets the signal current available to the subsequent
transimpedance amplifier.

The \emph{internal quantum efficiency} (IQE) $\eta_{\mathrm{int}}$ isolates the
carrier collection process from the optical coupling and absorption steps:
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{\mathrm{int}}
    = \frac{I_{\mathrm{ph}}/q}{P_{\mathrm{abs}}/\hbar\omega},
    \label{eq:iqe}
\end{equation}
where $P_{\mathrm{abs}}$ is the optical power actually absorbed within the Ge
layer and $\hbar\omega$ is the photon energy. A device with high EQE may still
exhibit a depressed IQE if significant absorption occurs in passive regions from
which carriers cannot be extracted, or conversely may have high IQE but low EQE
due to poor optical coupling. Distinguishing the two is important for correctly
attributing performance shortfalls to either optical design (coupling and
absorption) or electrical design (carrier transport and collection).

In the fully depleted intrinsic Ge layer under reverse bias, the electric field
exceeds the carrier velocity saturation threshold throughout the absorber volume.
The resulting drift transit time $\tau_{\mathrm{tr}} = W_i / v_{\mathrm{d,sat},h}$,
governed by the slower hole saturation velocity $v_{\mathrm{d,sat},h}$, is
typically tens of picoseconds for intrinsic layer thicknesses $W_i$ in the
sub-\SI{500}{\nano\metre} regime. Because this is many orders of magnitude
shorter than the Shockley--Read--Hall (SRH) minority-carrier lifetime
$\tau_0 \sim \SI{1}{\nano\second}$ in device-grade epitaxial Ge, virtually all
photo-generated carriers are swept across the junction before recombination can
occur. The resulting carrier collection efficiency is:
\begin{equation}
    \xi \approx 1 - \frac{\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}}{\tau_0} \approx 1,
    \label{eq:collection_eff}
\end{equation}
confirming that the IQE is essentially unity in a properly depleted device,
provided the electric field is sufficiently uniform and the surface recombination
velocity at the Ge/Si heterojunction is kept low by careful interface
passivation.

\subsubsection{The EQE Chain and Its Design Implications}

The connection between the physical loss mechanisms and the measurable EQE is
made precise by the following chain product:
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{\mathrm{ext}}
    = (1 - R_{\mathrm{refl}})\,\eta_c\,\xi\,
      \bigl(1 - e^{-\alpha(\lambda)\,L}\bigr),
    \label{eq:qe_full}
\end{equation}
where each factor corresponds to a distinct physical stage. The term
$(1-R_{\mathrm{refl}})$ accounts for Fresnel reflection at the Si/Ge entry
facet, arising from the refractive index contrast ($n_{\mathrm{Si}}\approx3.48$,
$n_{\mathrm{Ge}}\approx4.27$ at \SI{1310}{\nano\metre}); this factor is
typically in the range $0.97$--$0.99$ for waveguide-integrated devices where the
optical mode is guided through a tapered transition rather than impinging on an
abrupt cleaved facet. The coupling efficiency $\eta_c$ quantifies the spatial
overlap between the mode profile of the silicon access waveguide and the
fundamental mode supported by the Ge absorber rib; its value is sensitive to
cross-sectional geometry, waveguide width mismatch, and the design of any
intermediate taper structure. The collection efficiency $\xi$ has already been
established as approximately unity under full depletion. Finally, the factor
$(1 - e^{-\alpha(\lambda) L})$ is the fraction of the guided optical power
absorbed over a propagation length $L$ through a medium with power absorption
coefficient $\alpha(\lambda)$; at \SI{1310}{\nano\metre}, the absorption
coefficient of Ge is $\alpha \approx \SI{3e3}{\centi\metre^{-1}}$, yielding
$1/e$ absorption lengths of approximately \SI{3}{\micro\metre}. For a
\SI{10}{\micro\metre}-long device, the single-pass absorption factor approaches
$0.95$, indicating that a compact absorber can achieve near-complete photon
collection without the need for cavity enhancement.

Understanding \cref{eq:qe_full} as a product of separable factors is
strategically important: it allows independent optimisation of the coupling
optics, the absorber geometry, and the carrier transport, without conflating
distinct physical mechanisms. It also reveals which term dominates the EQE
deficit in any particular design iteration, enabling targeted improvements.
For the waveguide-integrated, vertical PIN architecture considered throughout
this work, the dominant EQE loss is typically associated with $\eta_c < 1$
arising from mode mismatch at the Si-to-Ge transition, rather than from
incomplete absorption or finite carrier lifetime.

\subsection[Bandwidth: Transit-Time and RC Limits]{Bandwidth: Transit-Time and RC Limits}
\label{subsec:pd_bw}

\subsubsection{Overview of Bandwidth-Limiting Mechanisms}

The ability of a photodetector to faithfully follow a modulated optical signal
at high frequencies is characterised by its \mbox{3\,dB} electrical bandwidth
$f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}$. In a reverse-biased PIN photodiode, this bandwidth is
fundamentally limited by two physically distinct and largely independent
mechanisms: the finite time required for photo-generated carriers to traverse
the depletion region (the \emph{transit-time} limit), and the charging time of
the junction capacitance through the total series resistance (the \emph{RC}
limit). Both mechanisms produce a roll-off in the photocurrent frequency
response, and in the absence of deliberate inductive resonance peaking, the
combined bandwidth is well described by the additive-inverse-quadrature
expression~\cite{SalehTeich1991}:
\begin{equation}
    f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}
    = \left(\frac{1}{f_{\mathrm{tt}}^2}
      + \frac{1}{f_{\mathrm{RC}}^2}\right)^{-1/2}.
    \label{eq:bw_total}
\end{equation}
This form, which adds the power spectral contributions of two first-order-like
roll-offs in quadrature, correctly reflects the fact that both mechanisms must
be simultaneously satisfied: a device with $f_{\mathrm{tt}} \gg f_{\mathrm{RC}}$
is RC-limited, while one with $f_{\mathrm{RC}} \gg f_{\mathrm{tt}}$ is
transit-time-limited. The engineering challenge is to design the geometry such
that both limits coincide, maximising the overall bandwidth for a given device
footprint.

\paragraph{Transit-time limit.}
When an electron--hole pair is photo-generated within the depleted intrinsic
layer, both carriers begin drifting in opposite directions under the action of
the built-in and externally applied electric field. The photocurrent induced
in the external circuit is not instantaneous but persists until all carriers
have traversed the junction and been collected at their respective ohmic
contacts. For a spatially uniform generation profile and carriers moving at
their saturation velocities, the current impulse response is approximately
rectangular in time. The Fourier transform of a rectangular pulse of duration
$\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}$ is a sinc function, whose first zero occurs at
$f = 1/\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}$, giving a \mbox{3\,dB} frequency:
\begin{equation}
    f_{\mathrm{tt}} = \frac{0.45\,v_{\mathrm{d,sat},h}}{W_i},
    \label{eq:metrics_bw_tt}
\end{equation}
where $v_{\mathrm{d,sat},h} \approx \SI{4e6}{\centi\metre\per\second}$ is the
hole saturation velocity in Ge, which is the lower of the two carrier saturation
velocities ($v_{\mathrm{d,sat},e} \approx \SI{6e6}{\centi\metre\per\second}$),
and $W_i$ is the intrinsic layer thickness. The numerical prefactor $0.45$
accounts for the asymmetric bipolar velocity ratio
$v_{\mathrm{d,sat},e}/v_{\mathrm{d,sat},h} \approx 1.5$: because electrons
transit the junction approximately 50\,\% faster than holes, the effective
current impulse is not a perfect rectangle, and the actual sinc-like roll-off
shifts the \mbox{3\,dB} point relative to the symmetric-carrier prediction.

It is instructive to evaluate \cref{eq:bw_tt} for the intrinsic layer thickness
$W_i = \SI{350}{\nano\metre}$ adopted in this work:
\begin{equation}
    f_{\mathrm{tt}}
    = \frac{0.45 \times \SI{4e6}{\centi\metre\per\second}}
           {\SI{350e-7}{\centi\metre}}
    \approx \SI{51}{\giga\hertz}.
    \label{eq:ftt_numerical}
\end{equation}
This value is a conservative lower bound because \cref{eq:bw_tt} is derived
from the worst-case single-carrier response governed by the slower holes. In
practice, because electrons and holes simultaneously contribute to the
terminal current via their respective Ramo--Shockley induced charges, the
two-carrier response is broader than the single-carrier prediction by a factor
that depends on the velocity ratio. Furthermore, experimental devices benefit
from self-resonance due to the parasitic inductance of the on-electrode metal
interconnect $L_p$, which introduces a peaking effect that can extend the
usable bandwidth substantially beyond the sinc-function roll-off. Accounting
for both effects, the benchmark-calibrated transport ceiling for a
$W_i = \SI{350}{\nano\metre}$ device is in the \SIrange{110}{120}{\giga\hertz}
class, confirming that transit time is not the operative bandwidth limitation
at the target operating rate of \SI{100}{\giga\hertz}.

\paragraph{RC limit.}
The junction capacitance $C_j$ of the fully depleted intrinsic region is that
of a parallel-plate capacitor with plate separation $W_i$ and area $A_j =
W_m \times L$, where $W_m$ is the mesa width and $L$ is the device length
along the waveguide axis:
\begin{equation}
    C_j = \frac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Ge}}\,A_j}{W_i},
    \label{eq:cj}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Ge}} \approx 16.2$ is the relative permittivity of
germanium. The physical interpretation is immediate: a thinner depletion region
brings the charge sheets of the $p$-type and $n$-type contacts closer together,
increasing $C_j$, while a larger junction area increases the total stored charge
per unit voltage. Both effects are detrimental to bandwidth.

The RC-limited \mbox{3\,dB} bandwidth is determined by the $RC$ time constant
of the junction capacitance discharging through the total series resistance
$R_{\mathrm{tot}} = R_s + R_L$, where $R_s$ is the parasitic series resistance
of the contacts and access electrodes, and $R_L = \SI{50}{\ohm}$ is the
termination resistance of the external readout circuit:
\begin{equation}
    f_{\mathrm{RC}} = \frac{1}{2\pi R_{\mathrm{tot}}\,C_j}.
    \label{eq:metrics_bw_rc}
\end{equation}
In the vertical $n$-$i$-$p$ PIN geometry, the dominant contribution to $R_s$
arises from the lateral current path within the $p^{++}$-doped silicon platform
layer beneath the Ge mesa. Carriers generated within the Ge absorber are
collected through the Si layer, which has a finite sheet resistance determined
by its thickness $t_{\mathrm{Si}} = \SI{220}{\nano\metre}$ and the anode
contact geometry:
\begin{equation}
    R_{s,\mathrm{anode}} \approx \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{Si}}}{t_{\mathrm{Si}}}
    \cdot\frac{\ell_a}{w_a},
    \label{eq:rs_anode}
\end{equation}
where $\ell_a$ and $w_a$ are the effective current path length and width
through the platform, respectively. This resistance can be reduced by placing
the anode contacts as close as possible to the base of the Ge mesa, which is a
critical layout constraint that directly couples electrical performance to
lithographic resolution. The RC time constant is:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\mathrm{RC}} = R_{\mathrm{tot}}\,C_j,
    \label{eq:tau_rc}
\end{equation}
from which $f_{\mathrm{RC}} = (2\pi\tau_{\mathrm{RC}})^{-1}$.

\subsubsection{Optimal Intrinsic-Layer Thickness}

The most powerful design insight that follows from \cref{eq:bw_tt,eq:bw_rc}
is that the transit-time and RC bandwidths respond oppositely to changes in
$W_i$: increasing $W_i$ reduces $f_{\mathrm{tt}}$ (longer transit path) but
increases $f_{\mathrm{RC}}$ (lower capacitance per unit area). This suggests
the existence of an optimal thickness $W_i^*$ at which the two limits are
balanced and the total bandwidth $f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}$ is maximised. Setting
$f_{\mathrm{tt}}(W_i^*) = f_{\mathrm{RC}}(W_i^*)$ and substituting
\cref{eq:bw_tt,eq:bw_rc,eq:cj} yields:
\begin{equation}
    W_i^* = \sqrt{0.9\pi\,R_{\mathrm{tot}}\,\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Ge}}
             \,v_{\mathrm{d,sat},h}\,A_j}.
    \label{eq:wi_optimal}
\end{equation}
Substituting the device parameters $R_{\mathrm{tot}} = \SI{60}{\ohm}$,
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Ge}} = 16.2$, $v_{\mathrm{d,sat},h} = \SI{4e6}{\centi\metre\per\second}$,
and $A_j = \SI{6}{\micro\metre\squared}$ gives $W_i^* \approx \SI{310}{\nano\metre}$,
which is within \SI{11}{\percent} of the chosen $W_i = \SI{350}{\nano\metre}$,
confirming that the device is positioned close to the bandwidth optimum. The
modest upward departure from $W_i^*$ is deliberate: it marginally reduces
$C_j$ and the associated RC penalty while accepting a small transit-time
degradation, improving manufacturing tolerance for process variations in the
epitaxial layer thickness.

The expression \cref{eq:wi_optimal} reveals a fundamental scaling law of
considerable practical importance. Because $W_i^* \propto \sqrt{A_j}$, the
optimal intrinsic thickness scales as the square root of the junction area.
Consequently, shrinking the junction area by a factor of four (e.g., from
$A_j = \SI{6}{\micro\metre\squared}$ to \SI{1.5}{\micro\metre\squared}) allows
$W_i^*$ to be halved, which simultaneously raises $f_{\mathrm{tt}}$ and
$f_{\mathrm{RC}}$ at the new balanced optimum by approximately the same factor.
This scaling analysis underpins the miniaturisation strategy adopted in the
next-generation device designs discussed in \cref{sec:pd_design}: reducing the
device footprint, rather than simply increasing the reverse-bias voltage, is the
primary lever for pushing integrated Ge-on-Si photodetectors beyond the
\SI{100}{\giga\hertz} bandwidth regime while maintaining the responsivity needed
for \SI{400}{\giga bit\per\second} data-centre interconnect applications.

\subsubsection{Small-Signal Transfer Function and Inductive Peaking}

The photocurrent frequency response of the photodetector can be compactly
described by a factorised small-signal transfer function. The transit-time
contribution takes the form of a sinc function inherited from the rectangular
current impulse response, and the RC contribution is a single-pole low-pass
filter:
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{PD}}(f)
    = \mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{f}{f_{\mathrm{tt}}}\right)
    \cdot\frac{1}{1 + i\,f/f_{\mathrm{RC}}},
    \label{eq:H_pd}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{sinc}(x) = \sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)$. This simplified two-pole model
is adequate for rapid design screening and for gaining physical intuition, but
it neglects the reactive parasitics that are unavoidable in a realistic device
layout.

In a fully parameterised electrical model of the packaged photodetector, the
reverse-biased junction presents an impedance $Z_j(\omega) \approx (j\omega C_j)^{-1}$,
while the series branch carries the ohmic resistance $R_s$ and the parasitic
inductance $L_p$ of the metal electrode interconnect:
\begin{equation}
    Z_s(\omega) = R_s + j\omega L_p.
    \label{eq:zs}
\end{equation}
The output is terminated by the load $Z_L(\omega) = (R_L^{-1} + j\omega C_p)^{-1}$,
where $C_p$ is the parasitic pad capacitance. Current division between these
impedances yields the peaked electrical transimpedance:
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{elec,pk}}(\omega)
    = \frac{Z_j(\omega)\,Z_L(\omega)}
           {Z_j(\omega)+Z_s(\omega)+Z_L(\omega)}.
    \label{eq:helec_pk}
\end{equation}
The full benchmark-calibrated detector response, combining the transit-time
and electrical sub-network contributions, is:
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{PD,pk}}(\omega)
    = H_{\mathrm{tt}}(\omega)\,H_{\mathrm{elec,pk}}(\omega),
    \label{eq:H_pd_pk}
\end{equation}
where $H_{\mathrm{tt}}(\omega)$ is the Fourier transform of the two-carrier
impulse current response. The physically important feature of
\cref{eq:helec_pk} is that the series inductance $L_p$ resonates with $C_j$
at a frequency $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{L_p C_j}$, producing a local maximum in the
transimpedance magnitude at frequencies near $\omega_0$. This so-called
\emph{inductive peaking} extends the usable \mbox{3\,dB} bandwidth beyond the
RC-only prediction of \cref{eq:bw_rc} without requiring any reduction in $C_j$
or $R_s$, and explains why experimentally measured bandwidths routinely exceed
the estimates based on the simplified two-pole model. Equation~\cref{eq:H_pd}
is employed for geometry screening throughout this chapter; \cref{eq:H_pd_pk}
is reserved for validated projections into the \SI{100}{\giga\hertz}-class
operating regime in \cref{sec:pd_design}.

\subsection[Noise, Sensitivity, and Figures of Merit]{Noise, Sensitivity, and Figures of Merit}
\label{subsec:pd_noise}

\subsubsection{Dark Current}
\label{subsubsec:pd_dark}

Even in the complete absence of optical excitation, a reverse-biased PIN
junction sustains a non-zero current, referred to as the \emph{dark current}
$I_d$. Far from being a mere background offset that can be electronically
subtracted, the dark current is a primary noise source: because current flow
involves the random transit of discrete charge carriers, it inevitably generates
shot noise, as formalised in \cref{subsec:pd_noise}. The dark current therefore
sets the fundamental noise floor of the detector and, through the
noise-equivalent power, determines the minimum detectable signal level.

Three generation-recombination mechanisms contribute to $I_d$ in a reverse-biased
Ge-on-Si PIN~\cite{SzeNg2006}. The first and generally dominant mechanism at
the device geometry considered here is \emph{thermal generation via SRH traps}
within the depleted intrinsic layer. In a reverse-biased junction, the carrier
concentrations are below their equilibrium values, and mid-gap traps act
predominantly as generation centres, thermally exciting carriers from the valence
band to the conduction band through an intermediate trap state. The generation
rate derived from the SRH formalism yields a current density:
\begin{equation}
    J_{\mathrm{SRH}} = \frac{q\,n_i\,W_i}{2\tau_0},
    \label{eq:metrics_jdark_srh}
\end{equation}
where $n_i$ is the intrinsic carrier concentration of Ge and $\tau_0$ is the
effective SRH carrier lifetime, which is related to the trap density and capture
cross-sections. Because $n_i$ in Ge is approximately $\SI{2.3e13}{\centi\metre^{-3}}$
at room temperature---nearly three orders of magnitude larger than in Si---the
SRH generation rate, and hence the dark current, is substantially higher in
Ge-based devices. This is a fundamental material limitation, not a fabrication
artefact, and it sets a floor on the achievable dark current density for any
Ge-on-Si photodetector at room temperature. The linear proportionality to $W_i$
in \cref{eq:jdark_srh} is another manifestation of the competing trade-offs
encountered throughout this section: thicker intrinsic layers improve
responsivity but simultaneously increase the dark current and its associated
noise contribution.

The second mechanism is \emph{minority-carrier diffusion} from the
quasi-neutral $p$-type and $n$-type contact regions flanking the depletion
layer. In these neutral regions, thermally generated minority carriers diffuse
toward the high-field depletion edge, where they are swept across by the electric
field. The diffusion current density from the $n$-type and $p$-type contact
regions is:
\begin{equation}
    J_{\mathrm{diff}}
    = \frac{qD_p\,n_i^2}{L_p\,N_D} + \frac{qD_n\,n_i^2}{L_n\,N_A},
    \label{eq:metrics_jdark_diff}
\end{equation}
where $D_p$ and $D_n$ are the minority-carrier diffusion coefficients, $L_p =
\sqrt{D_p\tau_p}$ and $L_n = \sqrt{D_n\tau_n}$ are the minority-carrier
diffusion lengths, and $N_D$ and $N_A$ are the donor and acceptor concentrations
in the respective contact regions. In a well-designed device with sufficiently
heavy doping in the contact regions, $J_{\mathrm{diff}}$ is suppressed below
$J_{\mathrm{SRH}}$ and is a secondary contribution to the total dark current.

The third mechanism, which becomes progressively more important as the device
is scaled to smaller dimensions, is \emph{interface recombination} (or,
equivalently, surface generation) at the Ge/Si heterojunction. The
crystallographic mismatch between Ge and Si results in a high density of
interface states that can act as generation centres with an effective surface
recombination velocity $v_s$. The associated current density is:
\begin{equation}
    J_{\mathrm{surf}} \approx \frac{q\,n_i\,v_s}{2}.
    \label{eq:metrics_jdark_surf}
\end{equation}
Combining all three contributions, and distinguishing between the area-scaled
bulk terms and the perimeter-scaled surface term, the total dark current is:
\begin{equation}
    I_d = A_j\!\left(J_{\mathrm{SRH}} + J_{\mathrm{diff}}\right)
    + P_j\,\frac{q\,n_i\,v_s}{2},
    \label{eq:metrics_idark_decomposed}
\end{equation}
where $P_j = 2(W_m + L)$ is the perimeter of the Ge mesa junction. This
decomposition into area-scaling and perimeter-scaling contributions has a
direct and important consequence for device miniaturisation: as the junction
area $A_j$ is reduced---in pursuit of lower capacitance and higher
bandwidth---the ratio $A_j / P_j$ diminishes, and the fractional contribution
of the surface term to $I_d$ grows correspondingly. In the limit of a very
small device, the perimeter term can come to dominate the total dark current,
making the quality of the Ge/Si interface and the effectiveness of any surface
passivation layer critically important. This represents a fundamental scaling
tension in the co-optimisation of bandwidth and noise performance.

\subsubsection{Noise Spectral Densities and Noise-Equivalent Power}
\label{subsubsec:pd_noise_psd}


\paragraph{Physical origin of shot noise.}
The shot noise PSD follows from the Poissonian statistics of discrete
carrier arrivals at the terminal. Each photon-induced electron-hole pair
is generated independently, giving a white (frequency-independent) PSD:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\mathrm{shot}}(f) = 2q\!\left(I_d + I_{\mathrm{ph}}\right)
    \quad [\si{\ampere\squared\per\hertz}],
    \label{eq:shot}
\end{equation}
where the total current includes both dark and photocurrent contributions.
The factor of two arises from the one-sided spectral density convention.

\end{figure}

\paragraph{Thermal noise.}
Simultaneously, the load resistance $R_L$ that terminates the detector generates
Johnson--Nyquist thermal noise through the random thermal motion of conduction
electrons. Its one-sided PSD is:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\mathrm{thermal}}(f) = \frac{4kT}{R_L}
    \quad [\si{\ampere\squared\per\hertz}],
    \label{eq:thermal}
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the Boltzmann constant and $T$ is the absolute temperature.
Like shot noise, this is a white noise process. At room temperature and with
$R_L = \SI{50}{\ohm}$, the thermal noise current density is
$\sqrt{4kT/R_L} \approx \SI{18}{\pico\ampere\per\sqrt\hertz}$. A dark current
of $I_d = \SI{1}{\nano\ampere}$ produces a shot noise of
$\sqrt{2qI_d} \approx \SI{0.6}{\femto\ampere\per\sqrt\hertz}$, more than four
orders of magnitude below the thermal noise floor. This confirms that for the
device under study, at moderate reverse bias, the detector itself is not the
dominant noise source; the \SI{50}{\ohm} termination resistance---and by
extension the input noise of the transimpedance amplifier---governs the overall
sensitivity of the optical receiver front end.

\paragraph{Total noise and noise-equivalent power.}
Integrating the noise PSD over the electrical detection bandwidth $B_e$ gives
the total noise variance at the receiver input:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_n^2 = \left(S_{\mathrm{shot}} + S_{\mathrm{thermal}}\right) B_e
    \quad [\si{\ampere\squared}].
    \label{eq:sigma_n}
\end{equation}
The \emph{noise-equivalent power} (NEP) is defined as the minimum optical power
required at the detector input to produce a signal current equal to the
root-mean-square noise current, i.e., the signal-to-noise ratio of unity in
a unit bandwidth:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{NEP}
    = \frac{\sqrt{S_{\mathrm{shot}} + S_{\mathrm{thermal}}}}{\mathcal{R}}
    \quad [\si{\watt\per\sqrt\hertz}].
    \label{eq:metrics_nep}
\end{equation}
A lower NEP indicates a more sensitive detector. When the dark current is the
dominant noise source (the dark-current-limited regime, relevant at high
reverse bias or elevated temperature), the NEP is determined by the shot noise
of $I_d$ alone, and the \emph{specific detectivity} $D^*$ provides a
geometry-normalised figure of merit that facilitates comparison across devices
of different junction areas:
\begin{equation}
    D^*_{\mathrm{shot}}
    = \frac{\mathcal{R}\sqrt{A_j}}{\sqrt{2qI_d}}
    \quad [\si{\centi\metre\sqrt\hertz\per\watt}].
    \label{eq:dstar}
\end{equation}
The $\sqrt{A_j}$ normalisation reflects the fact that, for area-dominated dark
current ($I_d \propto A_j$), the shot noise grows as $\sqrt{A_j}$, so a larger
device is intrinsically noisier in proportion to its collection area.

\paragraph{Additional noise contributions.}
Several secondary noise processes deserve mention even if they do not dominate
in the \mbox{tens-of-GHz} band of interest. Generation-recombination noise
from an ensemble of SRH traps with characteristic lifetime $\tau_{\mathrm{gr}}$
has a Lorentzian PSD with a corner frequency $f_{\mathrm{gr}} = 1/(2\pi\tau_{\mathrm{gr}})$
that typically falls below \SI{1}{\mega\hertz} in device-grade Ge; it is
therefore negligible at high-speed modulation frequencies. Optical-source noise,
characterised by the relative intensity noise (RIN) of the laser transmitter,
enters as:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\mathrm{RIN}}(f) = \mathcal{R}^2\,P_{\mathrm{opt}}^2\,\mathrm{RIN}
    \quad [\si{\ampere\squared\per\hertz}],
    \label{eq:rin_noise}
\end{equation}
and is proportional to the \emph{square} of the photocurrent. At the high optical
power levels encountered in short-reach interconnects using directly-modulated
lasers with RIN~$\approx -140$\,dB/Hz, this contribution can become comparable
to or exceed the shot-noise limit, shifting the system from a shot-noise-limited
to a source-noise-limited regime. The distinction matters for link budget
analysis but does not alter the intrinsic detector noise floor characterised
by \cref{eq:shot,eq:thermal}.

It is also important to clarify that passive optical loss mechanisms---including
taper insertion loss, waveguide propagation loss, free-carrier absorption in
doped contact layers, Fresnel reflection, and incomplete carrier
collection---do not generate new electrical noise PSDs. Instead, they reduce the
useful signal $I_{\mathrm{ph}} = \mathcal{R}\,P_{\mathrm{opt}}$ while the noise
floor remains governed by the remaining shot and thermal terms, thereby degrading
the signal-to-noise ratio indirectly. The distinction between noise and loss is
fundamental to correct link budget accounting.

\subsection[The Speed--Efficiency--Noise Trade-off]{The Speed--Efficiency--Noise Trade-off Triangle}
\label{subsec:pd_tradeoffs}

The performance metrics derived in the preceding subsections are not free
parameters that may be independently optimised. They are coupled through the
shared physical parameters of the device geometry---principally the intrinsic
layer thickness $W_i$, the junction area $A_j$, and the applied reverse bias
$V_R$---into a multi-dimensional constrained optimisation space. Understanding
the structure of this space is prerequisite to the informed design choices
made in \cref{sec:pd_design}.

\paragraph{Responsivity--bandwidth trade-off.}
The most fundamental tension is between responsivity and bandwidth, and it
arises from two independent geometric mechanisms. First, consider the role of
$W_i$: increasing the intrinsic layer thickness improves photon absorption
(the factor $1 - e^{-\alpha W_i}$ in \cref{eq:qe_full} rises toward unity),
thereby improving $\eta_{\mathrm{ext}}$ and $\mathcal{R}$. Simultaneously,
the longer transit path through the thicker layer reduces $f_{\mathrm{tt}}$
via \cref{eq:bw_tt}. These competing dependences are mediated by the optimum
thickness $W_i^*$ of \cref{eq:wi_optimal}: operating below $W_i^*$ sacrifices
both responsivity and bandwidth (transit-time limited, small $f_{\mathrm{tt}}$
and low absorption), while operating above $W_i^*$ gains responsivity at the
cost of RC bandwidth (higher $C_j$). Second, consider the role of the device
length $L$: a longer device absorbs a larger fraction of the incident guided
power, improving $\mathcal{R}$, but also increases $A_j = W_m \times L$,
raising $C_j$ via \cref{eq:cj} and degrading $f_{\mathrm{RC}}$ via
\cref{eq:bw_rc}. There is therefore a maximum useful device length beyond which
the RC bandwidth penalty outweighs the responsivity benefit, and this trade-off
length also scales with $A_j$ through the same $\sqrt{A_j}$ dependence of
$W_i^*$. The product $\mathcal{R} \times f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}$ is a useful
scalar summary of this two-dimensional trade-off and is employed in the
benchmarking analysis of \cref{subsec:pd_benchmarks}.

\paragraph{Bias voltage and the noise axis.}
Reverse bias adds a third dimension to the trade-off space. Increasing $V_R$
raises the electric field throughout the depletion region, driving both carrier
species closer to their saturation velocities and reducing the transit time
$\tau_{\mathrm{tr}} = W_i / v_{\mathrm{d}}(E)$. This improves $f_{\mathrm{tt}}$
and, for devices in which the field is initially insufficient for velocity
saturation, can also increase $\xi$ and hence $\eta_{\mathrm{int}}$. The noise
cost, however, is non-trivial: higher reverse bias drives greater band bending
and widens any partially depleted regions, increasing the volume over which SRH
generation occurs and thereby raising $I_d$ through \cref{eq:jdark_srh}. The
resulting increase in shot noise degrades NEP via \cref{eq:nep}. In the context
of a data-centre optical link, this noise penalty must be weighed against the
bandwidth benefit: for receivers operating at or near the sensitivity limit, the
optimal bias point is the lowest voltage at which full velocity saturation is
achieved throughout the intrinsic layer, since additional bias beyond this point
increases noise without providing further bandwidth benefit.

\paragraph{Miniaturisation and the perimeter penalty.}
Reducing the junction area $A_j$ below the value required for absorption
saturation trades responsivity for bandwidth, as discussed above. An additional
subtlety, captured by \cref{eq:idark_decomposed}, is that miniaturisation also
shifts the dark-current composition from bulk-dominated to perimeter-dominated.
For the current device with $A_j = \SI{6}{\micro\metre\squared}$ and
$P_j = \SI{8}{\micro\metre}$, the area-to-perimeter ratio is
$A_j / P_j = \SI{0.75}{\micro\metre}$; reducing $A_j$ by a factor of four while
halving the perimeter reduces this ratio by a factor of two, potentially doubling
the relative contribution of surface generation. Whether this constitutes an
absolute increase in $I_d$ depends on the relative magnitude of $J_{\mathrm{SRH}}$
versus the effective interface recombination velocity $v_s$, which in turn
depends on the quality of the Ge/Si heterojunction and any passivation treatment.
This linkage between geometry, process quality, and noise performance makes the
design of sub-micrometre Ge-on-Si photodetectors for beyond-100\,Gb/s
applications a genuinely multi-physics optimisation problem, spanning optical,
electrical, and materials dimensions simultaneously.

The design rationale developed in \cref{sec:pd_design} maps the full
geometry-to-performance relationship quantitatively, identifies the Pareto
frontier of the vertical PIN architecture with respect to the
responsivity--bandwidth--noise triple trade-off, and positions the chosen device
geometry within it with respect to the requirements of the \mbox{400GBASE-DR4}
application target.
