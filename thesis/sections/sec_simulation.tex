\section[Simulation Methodology]{Multiphysics Simulation Methodology}
\label{sec:pd_simulation}

The device performance metrics established in \cref{sec:pd_metrics} are evaluated
through a coupled optical-electrical simulation chain whose output is anchored to
the experimental benchmark of Shi~\textit{et al.}~\cite{Shi2024}. This section
specifies each solver, its numerical settings, the data flow connecting the
stages, and the reduced-order checks used to compare the solver outputs with the
geometry-derived bandwidth and noise models implemented in MATLAB.

\subsection[Optical Simulation: FDTD]{Optical Simulation: Three-Dimensional FDTD}
\label{subsec:pd_fdtd}

The optical simulation solves the time-domain Maxwell's equations of
\cref{eq:maxwell_curl_e,eq:maxwell_curl_h} throughout a domain encompassing
the silicon input waveguide, the \SI{40}{\micro\metre} adiabatic taper, the
complete germanium absorber, and the surrounding SiO$_2$ cladding. The solver
(Lumerical FDTD Solutions 2020~R2) discretises the electromagnetic field on a
Yee staggered grid using the second-order Courant-stable leapfrog scheme.
Computational photonics methodology for FDTD-based device simulation, including
the algorithmic implementation of the Yee algorithm and absorbing boundary
conditions, is detailed in the pedagogical treatment of Wartak~\cite{Wartak2013}.

The complex permittivity is sampled at the material-dependent optical frequency
through tabulated complex refractive indices. The auxiliary MATLAB response
model uses $\alpha\approx\SI{7e3}{\per\centi\metre}$ at
\SI{1310}{\nano\metre} for analytical responsivity estimates, while the FDTD
script evaluates absorption directly from the Palik material model.

\paragraph{FDTD source field and the $\varepsilon''$-based generation rate.}
Following the convention of Hamadou~\textit{et al.}~\cite{Hamadou2023}, the
monochromatic optical source in the FDTD domain is a Gaussian-modulated pulse:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{r},t)
    = E_0\exp\!\left[-\frac{(t-t_0)^2}{2\Delta t^2}\right]
      \sin\!\bigl[-\omega(t-t_0)\bigr]\,\hat{\mathbf{x}},
    \label{eq:fdtd_source}
\end{equation}
where $t_0$ is the temporal offset and $\Delta t$ is related to the
pulse FWHM by $\Delta t=\mathrm{FWHM}/(2\sqrt{\ln 2})$.
After the time-domain solution is Fourier-transformed, the volumetric
optical generation rate extracted from the monochromatic field is:
\begin{equation}
    G(\mathbf{r},\omega)
    = \frac{\pi\,\varepsilon_0\,\varepsilon''_r(\omega)}{h}
      \,|\tilde{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{r},\omega)|^2,
    \label{eq:gen_rate_eps}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon''_r(\omega)=2n\kappa$ is the imaginary part of the relative
permittivity and $h$ is Planck's constant~\cite{Hamadou2023}.
\Cref{eq:gen_rate_eps} is equivalent to \cref{eq:gopt_poynting_local} under
the plane-wave approximation, and reduces to \cref{eq:gen_rate} in the
uniform-field limit; the FDTD solver evaluates it pointwise over the
three-dimensional Ge volume before transferring the resulting
$G(\mathbf{r})$ array to the CHARGE drift-diffusion solver.
Frequency-domain power monitors record the input-normalised transmission before
and after the Ge absorber, from which the script computes:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{A}(\omega)
    = \max\!\left[\frac{T_{\mathrm{ref}}(\omega)-T_{\mathrm{after}}(\omega)}
                        {T_{\mathrm{ref}}(\omega)},\,0\right].
    \label{eq:fdtd_abs}
\end{equation}
A \SI{20}{\nano\metre} isotropic mesh override within the germanium region
resolves the exponential generation-rate gradient.

\paragraph{FDTD numerical stability and the 3D Courant condition.}
The PML implementation employs eight absorbing layers on each domain boundary,
matching the setting in \texttt{ge\_pd\_fdtd\_oband\_ushaped.lsf}. The temporal stability
of the Yee algorithm requires the 3D Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy condition:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta t < \frac{\Delta}{c\sqrt{3}},
    \label{eq:cfl_3d}
\end{equation}
so the time step is set to $\Delta t=0.99\,\Delta/(c\sqrt{3})$. The spatial
mesh size is independently constrained by the sampling requirement
$\Delta<\lambda_{\min}/(10\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\max}})$, giving
$\Delta<\SI{32}{\nano\metre}$ with a safety margin of 1.6 at the
\SI{20}{\nano\metre} override.

\paragraph{FDTD electromagnetic solution and the square-law photocurrent.}
A physically essential clarification connecting the FDTD solution to the
photocurrent calculation concerns what the power monitors extract. The
frequency-domain power recorded is the time-averaged Poynting vector
$\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r},\omega)=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*]$,
which is quadratic in the field amplitudes, consistent with the square-law
mechanism of \cref{eq:square_law}. The optical generation rate
$G_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{r})=\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{abs}}(\mathbf{r})/\hbar\omega$
of \cref{eq:gen_rate} is thus already expressed in the power-quadratic form that
maps directly to the photocurrent via $I_{\mathrm{ph}}=q\int_\Omega G_{\mathrm{opt}}\,\mathrm{d}V$~\cite{Pilori2019}.

\paragraph{Waveguide insertion loss and chip-level responsivity correction.}
The saturation of $\mathcal{A}(L)$ identified in the length sweep is not
smooth when waveguide routing loss is accounted for. For a Si ridge waveguide
with propagation loss $\gamma_{\mathrm{wg}}\approx\SI{3.6}{\decibel\per\centi\metre}$
at \SI{1310}{\nano\metre}, a routing length of $\ell_{\mathrm{wg}}=\SI{1}{\milli\metre}$
introduces an approximately \SI{0.36}{\decibel} correction equivalent to a
\SI{4}{\percent} responsivity reduction relative to the taper-input
value~\cite{Wang2024}. The FDTD domain of the present work terminates at the
taper entry; any comparison with chip-level measurements must apply the factor
$10^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{wg}}\ell_{\mathrm{wg}}/10}$ to \cref{eq:responsivity}.

\subsection[Electrical Simulation: CHARGE]{Electrical Simulation: Two-Dimensional Drift-Diffusion}
\label{subsec:pd_charge}

Carrier transport is simulated in Lumerical DEVICE CHARGE under
two-dimensional X-normal steady-state conditions with the norm length set to
the Ge absorber length $L=\SI{8}{\micro\metre}$. The solver evaluates the
vertical cross-section and scales currents by this longitudinal length, solving
the PDE system of
\cref{eq:poisson,eq:Jn,eq:Jp,eq:cont_n,eq:cont_p} on an adaptive
finite-element mesh. Two complementary carrier mobility models are employed.
The \emph{Caughey-Thomas} model accounts for impurity scattering through a
doping-concentration-dependent low-field mobility~\cite{Hamadou2023}:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{n,p}(N_A+N_D)
    = \mu^{\min}_{n,p}
      + \frac{\mu^L_{n,p} - \mu^{\min}_{n,p}}
             {1 + \left(\dfrac{N_A+N_D}{N^0_{n,p}}\right)^{\!\alpha_{n,p}}},
    \label{eq:sim_caughey_thomas}
\end{equation}
where $\mu^L_{n,p}$ is the lattice-scattering-limited mobility, $\mu^{\min}_{n,p}$
the ionised-impurity saturation floor, and $N^0_{n,p}$, $\alpha_{n,p}$ fitting
parameters. High-field velocity saturation is then superimposed through a
monotonic saturation model~\cite{Hamadou2023}:
\begin{equation}
    \mu'_{n,p}(|\mathbf{E}|)
    = \frac{\mu_{n,p}}
           {\left[1
             + \left(\dfrac{\mu_{n,p}|\mathbf{E}|}{v^S_{n,p}}
               \right)^{\!\beta_{n,p}}
           \right]^{\!1/\beta_{n,p}}},
    \label{eq:highfield_mob}
\end{equation}
yielding the field-dependent diffusivity $D_{n,p}=\mu'_{n,p}k_BT/q$ from
\cref{eq:einstein}. \Cref{eq:highfield_mob} is equivalent in functional form
to the Canali velocity-saturation model~\cite{Alasio2024}:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{n,p}(|\mathbf{E}|)
    = \frac{(\alpha_{\mathrm{C}}+1)\,\mu_{n,p}^{(0)}}
      {\left[\alpha_{\mathrm{C}}
        + \!\left(1 + \!\left(\frac{(\alpha_{\mathrm{C}}+1)\,
          \mu_{n,p}^{(0)}\,|\mathbf{E}|}{v_{\mathrm{d,sat}}}\right)^{\!\beta}
        \right)^{1/\beta}\right]},
    \label{eq:canali_full}
\end{equation}
which reduces to the saturation model of \cref{eq:mobility_sat} with $\beta=2$
and $\alpha_{\mathrm{C}}=0$. The low-field mobilities adopted are
$\mu_n^{(0)}=\SI{3900}{\centi\metre\squared\per\volt\per\second}$ and
$\mu_p^{(0)}=\SI{1900}{\centi\metre\squared\per\volt\per\second}$; the
saturation velocities
$v_{\mathrm{d,sat},e}=\SI{6e6}{\centi\metre\per\second}$ and
$v_{\mathrm{d,sat},h}=\SI{4e6}{\centi\metre\per\second}$ enter
\cref{eq:canali_full} as asymptotic limits. A $\pm20\%$ variation in
$\mu_p^{(0)}$ changes $f_{\mathrm{tt}}$ by less than \SI{1}{\percent},
confirming the robustness of the transit-time estimate in \cref{eq:bw_tt}
to low-field mobility uncertainty~\cite{Alasio2024}.

\paragraph{Junction capacitance and the microhole filling factor.}
For a planar junction the parallel-plate capacitance follows \cref{eq:cj}.
If photon-trapping microholes of total area $A_h$ are etched through the
junction region, the effective junction area is reduced by the filling
factor~\cite{Hamadou2023}:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_f = \frac{A_h}{A_j},
    \quad
    C_{j,\mathrm{mh}}
    = \frac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r\,A_j}{W_i}\,(1-\rho_f),
    \label{eq:cj_filling}
\end{equation}
so that microholes simultaneously improve absorption efficiency and reduce
capacitance, at the cost of increased surface recombination along the
etched sidewalls through \cref{eq:jdark_surf}. The present Ge-on-Si design
does not employ microholes; \cref{eq:cj_filling} is retained as a reference
for future microstructure extensions.

\begin{equation}
    \mu_{n,p}(|\mathbf{E}|) = \frac{\mu_{n,p}^{(0)}}
    {\left[1 + \left(\mu_{n,p}^{(0)}|\mathbf{E}|/v_{\mathrm{d,sat},{n,h}}\right)^{\beta}\right]^{1/\beta}},
    \label{eq:mobility_sat}
\end{equation}
SRH recombination is activated in the germanium absorber with calibrated
lifetimes $\tau_n=\tau_p=\SI{1}{\nano\second}$ in the current repository
configuration. Ohmic boundary conditions are imposed at the cathode
($V=0$) and the anode, which is swept from \SI{-2.5}{\volt} to
\SI{0.5}{\volt} over 51 points. The dark sweep is run first; the FDTD
generation-rate dataset is then imported and the illuminated sweep is repeated.
The same CHARGE setup also runs an SSAC frequency sweep from \SI{1}{\mega\hertz}
to \SI{200}{\giga\hertz}. All performance metrics are
extracted directly from the CHARGE solution: $\mathcal{R}$ from
\cref{eq:responsivity}; $\eta_{\mathrm{int}}$ and $\eta_{\mathrm{ext}}$ from
\cref{eq:qe_full}; $C_j$ from the small-signal AC analysis; and
$f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}$ from \cref{eq:bw_total} using the extracted $C_j$ and
modelled $R_s$ from \cref{eq:rs_anode}.

\subsection[System-Level Post-Processing: MATLAB]{System-Level Post-Processing: MATLAB}
\label{subsec:pd_matlab}

The MATLAB post-processing pipeline
(\texttt{system-level/ge\_pd\_dsp\_model.m} and
\texttt{device-level/ge\_pd\_oband\_ushaped\_postprocess.m}) consumes the CHARGE
output to compute the system-level performance projections. The key computations
are: (i) the combined transfer function from \cref{eq:bw_total,eq:H_pd_pk}
applied to the extracted $C_j$, modelled $R_s$, and the benchmark-calibrated
peaking parameters $(L_p,C_p)$; (ii) the sensitivity estimate from
\cref{eq:sens_full} at the projected bandwidth; and (iii) the parametric design
sweeps (absorber length and $W_i$ thickness) that produce the trade-off tables
and design-space visualisations.

\subsection[Validation Approach]{Validation Approach and Reference Design}
\label{subsec:pd_validation}

Validation is performed by comparing three levels of the same implemented
pipeline. First, the FDTD absorption monitor is checked against the expected
Beer-Lambert trend over the Ge-length sweep generated by
\texttt{ge\_pd\_fdtd\_oband\_ushaped\_sweeps.lsf}. Second, the CHARGE dark and
illuminated sweeps are reduced to $I_d$, $I_{\mathrm{ph}}$, $\mathcal{R}$,
EQE, and IQE by \texttt{ge\_pd\_oband\_ushaped\_postprocess.m}. Third, the
geometry-derived RC and transit-time model is evaluated from the same dimensions
and compared with the optional SSAC response exported by CHARGE. This validation
does not claim an implemented physics-informed neural-network surrogate; such a
surrogate remains a future extension rather than part of the present repository.

\subsection[Carrier Transport Physics]{Carrier Transport Physics in the Germanium Absorber}
\label{subsec:pd_transport_physics}

The electrical response of the Ge-on-Si photodetector is governed by the
coupled transport of photogenerated electrons and holes through the germanium
absorber under an applied reverse bias. Understanding the physical mechanisms
that drive this transport---and the material parameters that control
it---provides the physical foundation for interpreting the drift-diffusion
simulation results of \cref{subsec:pd_charge} and for rationalising the
bandwidth and responsivity trade-offs that emerge from the design-space sweeps
of \cref{subsec:pd_matlab}.

\subsubsection[Drift and Diffusion in a Reverse-Biased PIN Junction]%
              {Drift and Diffusion in a Reverse-Biased PIN Junction}
\label{subsubsec:drift_diffusion_physics}

Carrier transport in the intrinsic germanium layer is described by the
drift-diffusion formalism, in which each carrier species responds to both the
macroscopic electric field $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})$ and its own concentration
gradient. The electron and hole current densities are, respectively:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{J}_n = q\mu_n n\,\mathbf{E} + qD_n\,\nabla n,
    \qquad
    \mathbf{J}_p = q\mu_p p\,\mathbf{E} - qD_p\,\nabla p,
    \label{eq:jn_jp_full}
\end{equation}
where $n$ and $p$ are the electron and hole concentrations, $\mu_{n,p}$ are
the corresponding carrier mobilities, and $D_{n,p}$ are the ambipolar diffusion
coefficients related to the mobility by the Einstein relation:
\begin{equation}
    D_{n,p} = \frac{\mu_{n,p}\,k_B T}{q},
    \label{eq:sim_einstein}
\end{equation}
with $k_B$ the Boltzmann constant, $T$ the lattice temperature, and $q$ the
elementary charge. Under the strong reverse bias typical of high-speed
operation ($|V| \gtrsim \SI{1}{\volt}$), the depletion approximation holds
throughout the intrinsic Ge layer: the equilibrium carrier concentrations are
negligibly small compared to the photogenerated densities, and the electric
field is large enough that drift dominates over diffusion. Physically, this
means photogenerated electron--hole pairs are swept apart by the built-in and
applied field before they can recombine, a condition essential for both high
quantum efficiency and fast temporal response.

The dominance of drift over diffusion can be quantified by comparing the drift
length $\ell_{\mathrm{drift}} = \mu_{n,p}\tau_{n,p}|\mathbf{E}|$ to the
diffusion length $L_{n,p} = \sqrt{D_{n,p}\tau_{n,p}}$, where $\tau_{n,p}$ are
the minority carrier lifetimes. With $\tau_0 = \SI{1}{\nano\second}$, matching
the Ge lifetime configured in \texttt{ge\_pd\_charge\_oband\_ushaped.lsf}, and
$|\mathbf{E}| \sim \SI{e4}{\volt\per\centi\metre}$ across a
$W_i = \SI{1}{\micro\metre}$ intrinsic layer at \SI{-1}{\volt}, the drift length
is orders of magnitude larger than the absorber thickness for electron
mobilities near
$\mu_n^{(0)} = \SI{3900}{\centi\metre\squared\per\volt\per\second}$. This
confirms that the photogenerated
carriers transit the intrinsic layer on a timescale ($\sim \SI{10}{\pico\second}$)
much shorter than their recombination lifetime, and that the drift-diffusion
equations reduce, under these conditions, to a field-dominated transport problem
well suited to the finite-element CHARGE solver.

\subsubsection[Velocity Saturation and High-Field Carrier Dynamics]%
              {Velocity Saturation and High-Field Carrier Dynamics}
\label{subsubsec:velocity_saturation}

At the field magnitudes present inside a reverse-biased germanium absorber,
carriers cannot be treated as low-field linear responders. As the electric
field increases beyond a material-specific threshold, the carrier drift velocity
departs from its ohmic limit $v = \mu_{n,p}^{(0)}|\mathbf{E}|$ and saturates
at the material saturation velocity $v_{\mathrm{d,sat}}$, owing to the onset
of optical phonon emission, intervalley scattering (in the conduction band),
and momentum relaxation via high-energy phonon modes. In germanium, the
saturation velocities for electrons and holes are
$v_{\mathrm{d,sat},e} \approx \SI{6e6}{\centi\metre\per\second}$ and
$v_{\mathrm{d,sat},h} \approx \SI{4e6}{\centi\metre\per\second}$, respectively,
with the asymmetry arising from the differing effective masses and phonon
scattering cross-sections of the two carrier species.

The field-dependent mobility that captures this saturation behaviour is given
by the empirical model of \cref{eq:mobility_sat}, restated here for clarity:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{n,p}(|\mathbf{E}|)
    = \frac{\mu_{n,p}^{(0)}}
      {\!\left[1 + \!\left(
          \dfrac{\mu_{n,p}^{(0)}\,|\mathbf{E}|}{v_{\mathrm{d,sat},n/h}}
        \right)^{\!\beta}\right]^{\!1/\beta}},
    \label{eq:mobility_sat_body}
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is a fitting exponent that controls the sharpness of the
transition from linear to saturated transport: $\beta = 1$ corresponds to a
smooth asymptotic saturation, while $\beta \to \infty$ approaches the
idealised piecewise-linear two-piece model. For germanium, values in the range
$\beta \approx 1.6$--$2.0$ are typically adopted. The Canali model of
\cref{eq:canali_full}, which reduces to \cref{eq:mobility_sat_body} with
$\beta = 2$ and $\alpha_{\mathrm{C}} = 0$, is the formulation implemented in
the CHARGE solver and is equivalent in physical content to
\cref{eq:mobility_sat_body} within the parameter range of interest.

The engineering consequence of velocity saturation is immediately apparent from
the transit-time contribution to device bandwidth. The carrier transit time
across an intrinsic layer of thickness $W_i$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{n,p}^{\mathrm{tr}} = \frac{W_i}{v_{n,p}(|\mathbf{E}|)},
    \label{eq:tau_transit}
\end{equation}
where $v_{n,p}(|\mathbf{E}|) = \mu_{n,p}(|\mathbf{E}|)\,|\mathbf{E}|$ is the
drift velocity at the field magnitude sustained across the layer. In the
saturated regime, $v_{n,p} \to v_{\mathrm{d,sat},n/h}$ and the transit time
becomes independent of the applied bias, reducing to
$\tau_{n,p}^{\mathrm{tr}} \to W_i / v_{\mathrm{d,sat}}$. This saturation has
a critical design implication: beyond a certain applied reverse voltage, further
increasing the bias voltage does not meaningfully reduce the transit time, and
the transit-time-limited bandwidth component
\begin{equation}
    f_{\mathrm{tt}} = \frac{0.45}{\tau_{\mathrm{tr,eff}}}
    \approx \frac{0.45\,v_{\mathrm{d,sat,eff}}}{W_i}
    \label{eq:sim_bw_tt}
\end{equation}
reaches a plateau governed solely by the material saturation velocity and the
absorber thickness $W_i$. Here, $v_{\mathrm{d,sat,eff}}$ is an effective
average accounting for the asymmetric electron and hole velocities: because
electrons are faster in Ge ($v_{\mathrm{d,sat},e}/v_{\mathrm{d,sat},h} = 1.5$),
the hole transit time is the bandwidth-limiting process, and the design is
therefore sensitive to the choice of $W_i$ primarily through the hole dynamics.

\subsubsection[Doping-Dependent Mobility and Impurity Scattering]%
              {Doping-Dependent Mobility and Impurity Scattering}
\label{subsubsec:impurity_scattering}

While velocity saturation governs the high-field interior of the intrinsic
layer, the doped contact regions of the PIN structure are dominated by a
complementary mechanism: ionised impurity scattering. Each ionised dopant atom
introduces a long-range Coulomb perturbation that deflects carrier trajectories,
reducing the low-field mobility below its lattice-scattering limit. The
Caughey--Thomas model of \cref{eq:caughey_thomas} captures this reduction
through a smooth interpolation between the high-doping saturation floor
$\mu_{n,p}^{\min}$ and the lattice-limited ceiling $\mu_{n,p}^L$:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{n,p}(N_A + N_D)
    = \mu_{n,p}^{\min}
      + \frac{\mu_{n,p}^L - \mu_{n,p}^{\min}}
             {1 + \left(\dfrac{N_A + N_D}{N_{n,p}^0}\right)^{\!\alpha_{n,p}}},
    \label{eq:caughey_thomas_body}
\end{equation}
where $N_{n,p}^0$ is a reference doping concentration at which the mobility
lies midway between its limits, and $\alpha_{n,p}$ is a dimensionless exponent
characterising the sensitivity of scattering to doping level.

Physically, \cref{eq:caughey_thomas_body} reflects the fact that at low doping
concentrations, inter-impurity distances are large and carrier--impurity
collisions are rare, so mobility approaches the lattice-phonon-limited value.
At high doping ($N_A + N_D \gg N_{n,p}^0$), the density of scattering centres
is sufficient to dominate over phonon scattering, and the mobility saturates
at $\mu_{n,p}^{\min}$. In a practical Ge-on-Si PIN photodetector, the $p^+$ Ge
anode contact is typically doped to $\sim\SI{e18}{\per\centi\metre\cubed}$ and
the $n^+$ Si cathode to comparable levels; at these concentrations, the local
mobility is reduced by roughly a factor of three to five relative to the
intrinsic value.

From an engineering perspective, this mobility reduction in the contact regions
influences the series resistance $R_s$ of the device, which enters the RC-limited
bandwidth component alongside the junction capacitance $C_j$:
\begin{equation}
    f_{\mathrm{RC}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\,R_s\,C_j}.
    \label{eq:sim_bw_rc}
\end{equation}
Since $R_s$ is determined partly by the sheet resistance of the doped contact
layers---and hence by the mobility at the relevant doping level---a
self-consistent treatment of carrier mobility across the full doping range,
as provided by \cref{eq:caughey_thomas_body}, is essential for accurate
bandwidth prediction. Crucially, the high-field extension of
\cref{eq:mobility_sat_body} is applied on top of the Caughey--Thomas
low-field value, so the model transitions smoothly from impurity-dominated
scattering near the contacts to phonon- and field-dominated transport in the
intrinsic absorber, providing a physically complete description of carrier
dynamics throughout the device.

\subsubsection[Recombination Mechanisms and Dark Current Calibration]%
              {Recombination Mechanisms and Dark Current Calibration}
\label{subsubsec:recombination_dark}

In any semiconductor photodetector, photogenerated carriers compete with
recombination processes that act to annihilate electron--hole pairs before
they contribute to the external photocurrent. The dominant bulk recombination
mechanism in lightly doped germanium is Shockley--Read--Hall (SRH) recombination,
mediated by trap states located near the midgap of the germanium band structure.
These trap states arise from crystallographic defects---threading dislocations
and misfit dislocations generated by the approximately \SI{4.2}{\percent} lattice
mismatch between Ge and Si at the heteroepitaxial interface---and from residual
point defects introduced during growth and thermal processing.

The SRH net recombination rate is:
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{SRH}}
    = \frac{np - n_i^2}
      {\tau_p(n + n_1) + \tau_n(p + p_1)},
    \label{eq:srh_body}
\end{equation}
where $n_i$ is the intrinsic carrier concentration, $n_1 = n_i\exp[(E_t - E_i)/k_BT]$
and $p_1 = n_i\exp[(E_i - E_t)/k_BT]$ are the SRH reference concentrations
evaluated at the trap energy $E_t$ relative to the intrinsic level $E_i$, and
$\tau_n$, $\tau_p$ are the minority carrier lifetimes for electrons and holes
respectively. In the depletion approximation, $n \ll n_1$ and $p \ll p_1$,
so that \cref{eq:srh_body} reduces to:
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{SRH}}^{\mathrm{dep}}
    \approx -\frac{n_i}{\tau_0},
    \qquad
    \tau_0 = \frac{\tau_n + \tau_p}{2},
    \label{eq:sim_srh_dep}
\end{equation}
with the negative sign indicating net generation rather than recombination.
This generation current constitutes the dominant contribution to the device
dark current in reverse bias and is the quantity most directly affected by the
minority-carrier lifetime configured in the CHARGE simulation.

The current repository configures the Ge trap-assisted electron and hole
lifetimes as $\tau_n=\tau_p=\SI{1}{\nano\second}$. The final dark-current value
is therefore taken from the CHARGE output file rather than from an analytical
calibration inserted by hand. This choice lies within the
\SIrange{1}{10}{\nano\second} lifetime range commonly used for
dislocation-limited Ge-on-Si material. Any future
improvement in epitaxial quality---for example through a thicker graded buffer
layer or a hydrogen anneal---would manifest as an increase in $\tau_0$, reducing
the thermal generation component of the dark current per \cref{eq:srh_dep} and
improving the receiver sensitivity margin via \cref{eq:sens_full}.

\subsubsection[Physical Basis of the Bandwidth--Absorption Trade-off]%
              {Physical Basis of the Bandwidth--Absorption Trade-off}
\label{subsubsec:bw_absorption_tradeoff}

The coupled optical and electrical physics described in the preceding
subsections conspire to create the fundamental design tension that shapes all
waveguide-integrated photodetector optimisation: the trade-off between absorption
efficiency and bandwidth.

Absorption efficiency in the germanium absorber is determined by the overlap
of the evanescent optical mode with the Ge volume. For a waveguide-integrated
vertical PIN structure, the fraction of incident optical power absorbed over
an absorber length $L$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{A}(L) = 1 - \exp(-\Gamma\alpha L),
    \label{eq:absorption_efficiency}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma$ is the optical confinement factor in the Ge layer and
$\alpha \approx \SI{7e3}{\per\centi\metre}$ is the absorption coefficient used by
the auxiliary MATLAB response model at \SI{1310}{\nano\metre}. The FDTD script
computes absorption directly from the Palik material model. Increasing $L$
monotonically improves
$\mathcal{A}$; however, it simultaneously increases the junction area $A_j$
and therefore the junction capacitance $C_j = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A_j / W_i$.
Through \cref{eq:bw_rc}, this reduces $f_{\mathrm{RC}}$, degrading the
RC-limited bandwidth. Conversely, reducing $W_i$ to increase $|\mathbf{E}|$
for a given bias voltage---and thereby push carriers deeper into velocity
saturation---also raises $C_j$ through the inverse $W_i$ dependence. The
total $3\,\mathrm{dB}$ bandwidth is obtained by combining the transit-time and
RC contributions in quadrature:
\begin{equation}
    f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}
    = \left(\frac{1}{f_{\mathrm{tt}}^2} + \frac{1}{f_{\mathrm{RC}}^2}\right)^{\!-1/2},
    \label{eq:sim_bw_total}
\end{equation}
which makes explicit the fact that both $W_i$ and $L$ influence the bandwidth
through independent physical channels---$W_i$ through the transit time and
$L$ through the capacitance---and that neither can be optimised in isolation.

In the context of the O-band (\SI{1310}{\nano\metre}) target for
400GBASE-DR4 interconnects, the minimum bandwidth requirement is
$f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}} \geq \SI{50}{\giga\hertz}$ per lane, and the responsivity
must remain above approximately $\SI{0.8}{\ampere\per\watt}$ to maintain a
useful power budget. The parametric sweeps described in
\cref{subsec:pd_matlab} navigate this two-dimensional design space, with the
physical picture developed in the present section providing the interpretive
framework for understanding why specific combinations of $(L, W_i)$ lie on or
away from the Pareto frontier of the bandwidth--responsivity trade-off. The
transit-time plateau identified in \cref{subsubsec:velocity_saturation}
establishes the maximum achievable $f_{\mathrm{tt}}$ for a given absorber
thickness, and the RC ceiling set by the U-shaped anode geometry---through
its direct control of $R_s$ via \cref{eq:rs_anode}---determines how closely
the total bandwidth of \cref{eq:bw_total} can approach that transit-time
limit in the fabricated device.
